# Short narrated explainer videos: what the evidence says

Researched 2026-11-11 for the `blog-video` skill (`.claude/skills/blog-video/SKILL.md`). Scope: narrated motion-graphics explainers under 60 s, vertical 1080x1920, for YouTube Shorts, on programming topics.

Confidence labels: **High** means I opened the primary source and it says this. **Medium** means a primary source I could only read in part, or a credible secondary account of one. **Low** means secondary only, or my own inference. Where I could not open a source, the entry says so.

## Short answer

1. "The rule of 5 Ps" has no single owner and no evidence base. At least seven different lists use the name. Use a framework with a known source instead: And / But / Therefore for the script, SCQA to check it.
2. The best-supported design rules come from Richard Mayer's multimedia research: cut everything that does not serve the one idea (coherence, d = 0.86), point at what the voice is talking about (signaling, d = 0.70), and show a thing at the moment it is said (temporal contiguity, d = 1.31).
3. The case against burned-in captions is weak. Mayer's own 2021 figure for the redundancy effect is d = 0.10, and it shrinks or reverses for short text, second-language viewers and material without graphics. Keep captions, shorten what is shown at once.
4. The old finding that machine voices hurt learning (d = 0.74) comes from early-2000s synthesizers. Two studies with modern TTS found no learning difference from a human voice. Kokoro's own docs grade `bm_george` a C, though, and say voices are weakest on short clips.
5. YouTube publishes no "3 second" rule. It measures "Stayed to watch", the share of viewers who stay "past the initial seconds", and keeps the threshold private. The current video spends its first 2 s in silence.
6. URLs in Shorts descriptions and comments are not clickable. The only tappable link is a "related video" from the same channel. The typed URL in the outro has to work as something a person remembers.
7. No source I found makes a strong case for a music bed. The one controlled study on it found music hurt recall and transfer.
8. Biggest changes for the skill: speak from the first half second, script as hook / but / mechanism / therefore / pointer, tie a visible change to every sentence, and cap any scene at about 12 s.

## 1. Communication frameworks with clearer provenance

**And, But, Therefore (ABT).** Source: Randy Olson, *Houston, We Have a Narrative* (University of Chicago Press, 2015). The AAAS describes it as "adapted from the co-creators of ... South Park" and their rule of replacing "and" with "but" or "therefore" when editing scripts. [AAAS page](https://www.aaas.org/events/aristotle-south-park-abt-framework-new-tool-science-communication). Rule: state the setup (and), the problem (but), the consequence (therefore). Evidence: none experimental that I found; it is a craft rule. For a 50 s script: one sentence of setup, a "but" by second 8, the mechanism, then a "therefore" that the viewer could repeat to a colleague. A script that is all "and" (fact, and fact, and fact) is the failure it exists to catch. Confidence in attribution: **High**. I did not open the book.

**Pyramid principle and SCQA.** Source: Barbara Minto, *The Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing and Thinking*, developed at McKinsey in the 1970s, book editions from the 1980s. Rule: answer first, then the supporting points; open with Situation, Complication, Question, Answer. For a 50 s script: the punch line already is the Question. SCQA says the Answer should follow fast and the rest should support it, which argues for stating the answer in one line around second 10 and spending the middle showing why it is true. Confidence: **Medium**. Book not opened; details from secondary summaries, and it is a consulting method without experimental backing.

**Monroe's motivated sequence.** Source: Alan H. Monroe, *Principles and Types of Speech* (1935). Rule: Attention, Need, Satisfaction, Visualization, Action. Evidence: open textbooks that teach it say almost no research shows it persuades better than other structures. The one experiment they cite (Micciche, Pryor and Butler) found it was rated as more organized and no more persuasive. [Maricopa open text](https://open.maricopa.edu/com225/chapter/mms-need-new-list-included/). For a 50 s script: it is built for persuasion, and these videos explain. Only the first and last steps carry over (get attention, end on an action). Confidence: **Medium**, secondary sources only.

**SUCCESs.** Source: Chip and Dan Heath, *Made to Stick* (Random House, 2007). Rule: sticky ideas are Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories. For a 50 s script it works as a checklist. Simple: one mechanism. Unexpected: the punch line. Concrete and credible: real commands and real numbers, which the skill already requires. Emotional and stories mostly do not apply to a search algorithm. Confidence: **Medium**. Book not opened.

**Inverted pyramid.** A newswriting convention from the late 19th century, most important fact first. The standard history is Horst Pöttker's 2003 paper in *Journalism Studies* (not opened, cited from memory). For a 50 s script: it conflicts with a question hook, because it gives the answer away in the first line. It fits feeds where people leave early. A workable mix is the question in the first 2 s and the one-line answer by about second 10. Confidence: **Low** on the citation, **High** that the convention exists.

Takeaway: ABT gives the spine, SCQA checks that a question is posed and answered, SUCCESs checks the content. None of them is evidence-based in the way section 2 is.

## 2. Evidence-based multimedia learning

Main source, opened and read: Richard E. Mayer, "Research-Based Principles for Designing Multimedia Instruction", which reports medians from his 2021 meta-analysis (*Multimedia Learning*, 3rd ed.). [PDF hosted by UNH](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf). Mayer treats d above 0.40 as educationally important. These studies test learning from lessons of a few minutes, mostly with students in labs, not retention on a feed. That gap is real and applies to everything in this section.

| Principle | Mayer's wording | Tests | Median d | What it means here |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Coherence | Delete extraneous material | 18 of 19 | 0.86 | One mechanism per video. No decorative motion, music or asides. Strongest when the lesson is system-paced, which a Short is. |
| Signaling | Highlight essential material | 26 of 28 | 0.70 | Turn the thing being spoken about green, draw the arrow, at that moment. Works best "when visual and verbal signals are used sparingly". |
| Redundancy | Don't add onscreen captions to narrated graphics | 8 of 12 | 0.10 | See below. |
| Spatial contiguity | Place printed words near the graphic they describe | 9 of 9 | 0.82 | Labels inside the diagram, as the `note` fields already do. |
| Temporal contiguity | Present spoken words at the same time as the graphic | 8 of 8 | 1.31 | The `at` cue system is the right idea. Every reveal should land on its sentence. |
| Segmenting | Break the lesson into learner-paced parts | 7 of 7 | 0.67 | A Short cannot be learner-paced. Short scenes with a visible boundary are the closest thing. The classic study used 10-second segments. |
| Pre-training | Teach names of key concepts first | 10 of 10 | 0.78 | Name the one unfamiliar term before using it (for the pilot: "chunk"). |
| Modality | Spoken words rather than printed, with graphics | 18 of 19 | 1.00 | Supports narration as the main verbal channel. Printed words can win when the text has technical terms or is in the viewer's second language. |
| Personalization | Conversational style rather than formal | 13 of 15 | 1.00 | The tests used "your lungs" against "the lungs". This is about addressing the viewer, which third-person narration can still do. |
| Voice | Appealing human voice rather than machine voice | 6 of 7 | 0.74 | See below. |
| Embodiment, image | On-screen agent gestures; a static agent image adds little (d = 0.19) | 16 of 17; 4 of 7 | 0.58; 0.19 | No presenter needed. |

Confidence in the table: **High** (read in the source).

Cognitive load theory (John Sweller) is the frame under most of this: working memory is small, so anything a viewer has to hold, search for or reconcile takes capacity from understanding. I cite it through Mayer's handout and did not open Sweller's papers.

### The caption tension, resolved as far as the evidence goes

What the redundancy principle says. Mayer's wording is narrow: "People do not learn more deeply when on-screen text is added to narrated graphics." The reasons given are that people try to reconcile two verbal streams, or read the words and miss the graphic. In his 2021 numbers it was supported in 8 of 12 tests with a median d of 0.10, the weakest principle on the list and far under his own 0.40 bar. **High**.

Its boundary conditions, quoted from the same handout: the effect "can be diminished or even reversed when the lesson is slow-paced or under learner control, the on-screen text is short or in a reworded form, the words are in the learner's second language, or the material lacks graphics." **High**.

Supporting studies:

- Mayer and Johnson (2008), "Revising the redundancy principle", *Journal of Educational Psychology* 100(2): adding two or three word labels taken from the narration, placed next to the part of the graphic they describe, improved retention and did not hurt transfer. **Medium**, abstract and secondary accounts only.
- Adesope and Nesbit (2012), meta-analysis, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 104(1): learners given spoken plus written words outperformed spoken-only learners overall, with the benefit depending on pacing, prior knowledge and whether pictures were present. **Medium**, abstract only; I did not get the subgroup numbers.
- Mayer, Lee and Peebles (2014), *Applied Cognitive Psychology*: for non-native English speakers, captions on a fast-paced video did not help comprehension. So "second-language viewers benefit" does not hold automatically at Shorts pace. **Medium**, abstract only.
- Gernsbacher (2015), "Video Captions Benefit Everyone": cites more than 100 studies linking captions to comprehension, attention and memory. Critics point out most of that body is about second-language learners, deaf or hard-of-hearing viewers and self-report. **Medium**.

What platform data says about muted viewing:

- The famous sound-off numbers come from Facebook's muted-autoplay feed (the "85% without sound" figure reported by Digiday in 2016) and from a 2019 Verizon Media and Publicis survey (69% watch with sound off in public, and 80% say they are more likely to finish a captioned video). I found only secondary coverage of both, and the 80% figure is reported two different ways. **Low**. Treat as "common practitioner claim, unverified".
- YouTube is a sound-on platform by Google's own account. A Google presentation cites "Google Internal Data, Global, Sept. 2018" for 95% of YouTube video being played with sound on. I saw this through a search snippet and could not open the Think with Google page, which now redirects. **Low to Medium**.
- Google's Shorts ads guide tells advertisers to "Use sound (music, voiceover, or both)" and to "consider using text overlays". It does not mention captions. [Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/16041697). **High**.
- YouTube auto-generates captions for Shorts and asks creators to review them or upload their own. [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6373554?hl=en). **High**. The skill's SRT covers this.

Resolution. Keep burned-in captions. The measured cost is small and sits exactly where the boundary conditions weaken it: these viewers include many second-language English readers, the script is full of technical terms, and some share of viewers will be muted. The cost that remains is split attention: a viewer reading a full sentence at the bottom is not looking at the diagram at the top. The evidence points to three mitigations: show less text at once (a phrase or clause, in step with the voice), keep it close to the visual it describes, and never start a new caption and a new block of on-screen reading in the same instant. No study I found tests phrase-by-phrase captions on short-form video directly, so this is inference from the boundary conditions. **Medium**.

### The voice principle and modern TTS

- The original effect (d = 0.74, 6 of 7 tests) comes from studies such as Mayer, Sobko and Mautone (2003), which used a machine-synthesized voice of that era. **High** for the numbers.
- Craig and Schroeder (2017, *Computers & Education*) compared a classic TTS engine, a modern one (NeoSpeech "Kate") and a recorded human, with an on-screen agent. The modern engine matched the human voice on learning and beat the classic engine. Their 2019 follow-up without an agent (*Journal of Educational Computing Research* 57(6)) found the human voice was rated higher for humanness and engagement, with no difference in learning outcomes. [ERIC record via search](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/eric__EJ1225297). **Medium**, abstracts only.
- Mayer accepts this in the handout: Craig and Schroeder "found that advances in modern text-to-speech engines can allow them to be more human-like." **High**.
- Liew and colleagues (2022, *Education and Information Technologies*, [doi:10.1007/s10639-022-11255-6](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/s10639-022-11255-6)): an enthusiastic Alexa voice improved learners' reported emotions and cognitive load against a neutral one, and did not improve retention or transfer. **Medium**.

Reading: a good neural voice probably costs nothing in understanding and something in liking. Perceived warmth matters for a channel even if test scores do not move. No study I found tested Kokoro.

## 3. YouTube's own guidance for Shorts

### What YouTube or Google states

- **Length.** Shorts are "short-form videos that are up to 3 minutes long"; titles max 100 characters; max resolution 1080p. [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/10059070). **High**.
- **The first seconds.** YouTube Analytics defines "Stayed to watch" as "The percentage of times viewers stayed to watch past the initial seconds of a Short", and "Engaged views" as "How many times viewers stayed to watch past the initial seconds, not including any loops." [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12220281). No number of seconds is given. **High**. "Viewed vs swiped away" is the older name for the same split; I could not find it on the current Help page.
- **Why the threshold is private.** In a 2023 Creator Insider interview, Shorts product lead Todd Sherman said a view should "encode for your intent of watching that thing", and that thresholds are not shared, to prevent gaming. He also said there is no ideal length (tell the story), no magic posting volume, hashtags are optional, and that Shorts viewers swipe through a feed where long-form viewers choose. [Search Engine Journal summary](https://searchenginejournal.com/youtube-explains-how-shorts-algorithm-works/494953), [video](https://youtu.be/n3jsYK_-aRU). **Medium**: I read the summary, not the video.
- **Views and loops.** Since 2025-03-31 a Shorts view counts each time the Short "starts to play or replay". The old stricter count lives on as engaged views, which still decide earnings and Partner Program eligibility. [TechCrunch quoting YouTube](https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/26/youtube-is-changing-how-youtube-shorts-views-are-counted). **Medium**. Since engaged views exclude loops, I found no YouTube statement that looping helps distribution.
- **Links.** From 2023-08-31, links in Shorts comments, Shorts descriptions and the vertical live feed are not clickable. [TechCrunch reporting YouTube's announcement](https://techcrunch.com/2023/08/10/youtube-is-disabling-links-on-shorts-to-cut-down-on-spam). **Medium**: I could not find YouTube's original post. Channel profile links near the Subscribe button stay clickable.
- **Related video.** "A related video allows you to showcase your Videos, Shorts, and Live in the Shorts player. Viewers will see a clickable link below your channel handle." It must be a video on the same channel, public or unlisted, and it needs advanced feature access. [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14075157). **High**. So a Short cannot link to ivankristianto.com. It can link to another video on the channel.
- **Thumbnails.** "You can choose a frame from your Short to be used as the thumbnail before and after you upload", only from the YouTube mobile app, and you can add text and filters. [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/10343433?hl=en). **High**. Sherman's reason: most Shorts traffic comes from the feed, where no thumbnail is shown.
- **Captions.** Auto-generated for Shorts; creators are asked to review or supply their own. [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6373554?hl=en). **High**.
- **Safe zones.** Google publishes safe-zone templates for vertical video ads, with the instruction to keep "important elements like your logo, product, supers, and others ... within the red safe area", and a warning that overlays "may appear in different places depending on the format, campaign type, and screen." [Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/13547298). The pixel values are inside an image I could not read. **High** that the template exists, **no data** on the numbers. I found no YouTube safe-zone spec for organic Shorts.
- **Shorts ads guidance** (ads, so only loosely transferable): vertical 9:16, keep it under 60 s, use sound, consider text overlays. [Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/16041697). **High**.
- **General titles and thumbnails.** "Make sure titles accurately reflect your video and get to the point quickly." [YouTube Creators](https://www.youtube.com/creators/how-things-work/content-creation-strategy/). **High**.

### What creators and marketing blogs claim (unverified)

- "You have 3 seconds" (also 1 to 2 s, also 0.3 to 0.5 s). No YouTube source. The numbers differ from blog to blog. YouTube only says "initial seconds".
- "A good Stayed to watch rate is 70 to 90%." Third-party benchmarks; YouTube publishes none.
- "Loop the ending into the opening for replays." Plausible for the public view count since 2025. No evidence it affects recommendation, and engaged views exclude loops.
- "Change the shot every 3 to 5 seconds." No platform source.
- Safe-zone pixel values (for example 48 px left, 192 px right, or a 900x1160 centre box). Third-party sites disagree with each other.
- "Humans now have an 8-second attention span, shorter than a goldfish." The BBC's Simon Maybin traced this in 2017 ("Busting the attention span myth") to a Microsoft Canada report that took it from a site called Statistic Brain, which could not point to any research. I could not open the BBC page from here; this is from several secondary accounts that agree. **Medium** that the number has no real source.

## 4. Craft practice from explainer makers

- **Kurzgesagt.** Their process page lists five phases in order: research, script, illustration, voiceover and animation, music and sound. The script is "the backbone of our videos" and "can take about a dozen drafts". The narrator's "voiceover provides the timing for the animation team". [kurzgesagt.org](https://kurzgesagt.org/youtube/). **High**. Their video "How to Make a Kurzgesagt Video in 1200 Hours" gives the hours figure; I saw it only through secondary transcripts. Lesson: script first, audio sets timing, which is how the skill already works.
- **3Blue1Brown (Grant Sanderson).** From his FAQ: "Every movement on the screen should be deliberate, with an identifiable purpose." Give examples before general frameworks. "Topic choice matters way more than production quality." [3blue1brown.com/faq](https://www.3blue1brown.com/faq). From the first Summer of Math Exposition results: what stood out was "clarity, strong motivation, compelling choices of topic matter, and non-obvious insights." [Post](https://www.3blue1brown.com/blog/some1-results). **High**. The first quote is close to the site's own motion rules.
- **Fireship (Jeff Delaney).** From his video "How I Make Videos for Programmers", read through a third-party transcript: "When I present a graphic on the screen, it typically only stays there for 1 to 5 seconds." "Try to keep long pauses to a minimum and give your viewer a consistent feed of useful information." "I record the audio in a bunch of small chunks, almost sentence by sentence." He says he does not write a full script for every video. [Transcript](https://gotranscript.com/public/how-i-built-a-successful-youtube-channel-with-programming-tutorials). **Medium**. This is the only concrete seconds-per-graphic number I found from a programming explainer maker.
- **Vox (Joss Fong).** The best source looked like The Open Notebook's 2020 interview about her black hole video. The site returned 403, so I have nothing first-hand. Nieman Lab reports that one producer owns a Vox video from pitch to publish and that Fong picks stories that work better as video than text (secondary, not opened).
- **Wendover, Polymatter, Johnny Harris, School of Motion.** I did not find first-party process write-ups with concrete numbers in the time I had. The "150 words per minute" figure that explainer studios repeat came up only on vendor blogs. Unverified.
- **Speaking rate, with data.** Guo, Kim and Rubin (2014), "How Video Production Affects Student Engagement", 6.9 million edX sessions: speaking rates ran 48 to 254 wpm, mean 156. "Students generally engaged more with videos where instructors spoke faster", and the 145 to 165 wpm bucket was the least engaging for 6 to 12 minute videos. The authors warn that rate is "merely a surface feature that correlates with enthusiasm" and that "speeding up an unenthusiastic instructor might not improve engagement." edX producers "edit out pauses and filler words". [Paper PDF](https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf). **High** (read). George at about 170 wpm is already above the mean. Turning the speed up is not supported.
- **Shot length in film.** Cutting, DeLong and Nothelfer (2010, *Psychological Science*) is the usual citation for average shot length falling to a few seconds in modern Hollywood films. I read the abstract only, and the specific "8 to 10 s down to 3 to 4 s" numbers came from secondary coverage. **Low**. It shows what audiences are used to. It does not show that faster is better for understanding.
- **Music and sound.** Moreno and Mayer (2000), "A coherence effect in multimedia learning: the case for minimizing irrelevant sounds", *Journal of Educational Psychology* 92(1): adding background music to a narrated animation hurt retention and transfer in both experiments; adding environmental sounds hurt in one of two. [PDF](https://tecfa.unige.ch/tecfa/teaching/methodo/Moreno_Mayer00.pdf). **Medium** (abstract and summary). Against that: Kurzgesagt and 3Blue1Brown both commission music, and Google's ads guidance says sound of some kind (voiceover counts) lifts ad conversions. Nobody I found has measured music's effect on Shorts retention.
- **Disney's twelve principles** (Thomas and Johnston, *The Illusion of Life*, 1981, not opened). Under a no-bounce, no-scale rule, the ones that still apply are timing, staging (one clear thing to look at), slow in and slow out (the template's ease-out), overlapping action (staggered reveals) and anticipation in a mild form (a box outline before its label). Squash and stretch, exaggeration and arcs do not apply.

## 5. TTS narration specifics (Kokoro)

Three layers matter here: the model (hexgrad/Kokoro-82M), the wrapper the skill uses (`kokoro-onnx` 0.6.1), and the phonemizer inside the wrapper (espeak, not misaki).

- **Clip length.** Kokoro's voice notes: "Most voices perform best on a 'goldilocks range' of 100-200 tokens out of ~500 possible." Weak on "short utterances, especially less than 10-20 tokens", and prone to rushing over 400. [VOICES.md](https://huggingface.co/hexgrad/Kokoro-82M/blob/main/VOICES.md). **High**. A token is roughly one phoneme character. The skill speaks one sentence per clip; the pilot's sentences are about 40 to 90 tokens, under the recommended range. The two-word test clip "It works." is about 10 tokens.
- **Voice grades.** Same page: `bm_george` has target quality B, 10 to 100 minutes of training audio, overall grade C. Other British male voices: `bm_fable` C, `bm_lewis` D+, `bm_daniel` D. Best British voice is `bf_emma` at B-. Best overall are `af_heart` (A) and `af_bella` (A-), both American female. The page adds that grades reflect training data and that "voices will sound better or worse to different people". **High**.
- **Speed.** `kokoro-onnx` accepts 0.5 to 2.0 (source: [`__init__.py`](https://github.com/thewh1teagle/kokoro-onnx/blob/main/src/kokoro_onnx/__init__.py)). Measured locally on one 11-word sentence with `bm_george`: 3.93 s at 0.9, 3.58 s at 1.0, 3.37 s at 1.1. **High**.
- **Punctuation.** The model's vocabulary has tokens for `; : , . ! ? … " ( )`, the em dash, and four arrows (`↓ → ↗ ↘`) (read from the installed `kokoro_onnx.config`). Measured locally: swapping a comma for nothing, a colon, an ellipsis or a dash moved the length of that sentence by at most 0.12 s, and "One search. Five results." came out 0.06 s longer than the comma version. So punctuation does not buy pauses in this pipeline. It may still change pitch contour, which I cannot hear. What the arrows do in English is undocumented. **High** for the measurements, **no data** for intonation.
- **Pauses.** `create()` has `sentence_pause=0.25` and `clause_pause=0.1`, but they apply between batches of a long text, and a single sentence is one batch. The reliable pause control is the one the skill already has: the gap inserted between clips (fixed at 0.35 s in `scripts/narrate.mjs`). **Medium** (signature read from the installed package, behaviour from the upstream source summary).
- **Pronunciation.** The upstream Kokoro and misaki READMEs show inline markup, `[Kokoro](/kˈOkəɹO/)`. [kokoro README](https://github.com/hexgrad/kokoro), [misaki README](https://github.com/hexgrad/misaki). It does not work in the skill's pipeline: `kokoro-onnx` phonemizes with espeak and read the markup aloud as "slash k stress o...". What does work is `create(..., is_phonemes=True)` with a phoneme string, which gives control of stress through `ˈ` (primary) and `ˌ` (secondary). British English uses `a`, `Q`, `ɒ` and `ː` where American uses `æ`, `O`, and `ɹ`. [EN_PHONES.md](https://github.com/hexgrad/misaki/blob/main/EN_PHONES.md). **High**. The `kokoro-onnx` README recommends misaki for G2P from v1.0 on.
- **Blending.** Supported: `get_voice_style()` returns an array, and a weighted sum of two arrays can be passed as `voice`. [with_blending.py](https://github.com/thewh1teagle/kokoro-onnx/blob/main/examples/with_blending.py). **High**. The repo also has `with_timestamps.py` and `with_continuous.py` examples, which I did not read; they may allow one clip per scene with caption timings, which would move clips into the recommended token range.
- **General prosody levers** (inference, not a cited finding): vary sentence length, put the important word last so it takes the sentence stress, and write numbers the way they should be said. The pilot's longest clip is 6.4 s for one sentence, mostly a spelled-out number.

## Recommendations for the blog-video skill

Ordered by expected effect. "Fits" means inside the fixed design rules (no music, no gradients, shadows or glows, one accent, system fonts, motion limited to fades, draw-ins, typed text and colour changes, third-person narration).

| # | Change | Test | Supported by | Design rules |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Start the voice on the intro card. ## Open questions for the owner

1. **Which "5 Ps"?** Where did the phrase come from (a course, a person, a video)? If it was a specific list, I can check it against the frameworks above. None of the seven I found has evidence behind it.
2. **Silent bookends.** Recommendations 1 and 7 put voice over the intro and outro. Are the silences a design choice to keep, or just how the first version was built?
3. **"You".** Does "narration is third person" rule out addressing the viewer as "you"? The personalization effect is large in Mayer's data (d = 1.00) and does not require first-person claims about Ivan.
4. **Music.** I found no strong case for a music bed, and one controlled study against it. I am not flagging it for reconsideration. If retention data from the first few Shorts says otherwise, that would be the evidence to revisit with.
5. **The voice.** Kokoro grades `bm_george` C and `bf_emma` B-, with `af_heart` at A. The research says a modern TTS voice probably does not hurt understanding, and that people like human-sounding voices more. Worth a blind listen of the same 50 s script in George, Emma and Heart, or a George and Fable blend?
6. **Where the Short should send people.** The post URL cannot be tapped from a Short. Options: rely on the typed URL and the channel profile link; or also publish a horizontal long-form cut on the channel and set it as the Short's related video, with the post link in that video's description (clickable there).
7. **Phrase captions.** Recommendation 6 needs clause or word timings. Two routes: split captions into clause clips (more, shorter clips, which Kokoro handles worse), or synthesize a scene in one pass and take timings from `kokoro-onnx`'s timestamp example or from Whisper. Which trade-off is acceptable needs a listen.
8. **Measuring it.** After upload, the numbers that test these changes are "Stayed to watch" and average percentage viewed in YouTube Analytics (Advanced Mode for engaged views). Should the skill's report include a reminder to record them per video?
